# Computational Physics - Project 2

We chose to do our project on the time-independent Schrodinger equation since we both had experience with the basics of quantum mechanics, and thought programmatically calculating the TISE would be both a good exercise in coding and a fitting follow-up to the previous project. We hoped to complete it by utilising what we’d individually learned from our prior work, ideally using portions of it where possible, while also building up new frameworks where necessary. Specifically, while we could utilise our Euler/RK4 code, we would need to modify it to work for multiple inputs, and we would need something new for solving the equation at the right hand side of the potential well to detect sign changes in order to find roots. From those roots, we would also need some new code to normalize our wavefunctions, which would allow us to obtain their true form.
        
Our first task was both the simplest and the most difficult: we had to figure out how we were going to approach the problem. On the surface, it’s not super difficult to solve the time-independent Schrodinger equation - it’s one of the first things we do in physics 13. However, this problem is more complicated than it seems because we don’t have to just solve the TISE for the infinite potential well, or some other easy potential function that we can easily solve for. Instead, we have to solve the TISE for any given potential function. To this end, we need to be able to take in a list of points and solve the TISE for each of them, giving us some resultant wavefunction that we can then obtain the energy level roots from. We opted for RK4 for this, and worked with Claude to develop an RK4 solver that could take in a set of points, operate on them with a given function to find their derivatives, and return that set of points. To this end, we manually solved the TISE using the stand-in “u” for dψ/dx, giving us an easy way to have a system of two first-order differential equations: dψ/dx = u, du/dx = 2(V(x) - E)ψ. Since we knew the previous values for ψ and u, we could then use our RK4 solver to essentially “iterate” along the step of size h and find the next ψ and u, then repeat the process. Of course, this required an initial value for both ψ and u; we chose 0 for ψ and 1 for u, resulting in all of our wavefunctions starting with a positive slope when graphing them. We also defaulted to a step size of h = 0.01 - this seemed to fit our needs sufficiently well, and we didn’t see a reason to change it.
        
Now, we could go ahead and solve this for every single possible E, but that would a) take a massive amount of processing power and b) not be particularly useful due to the wavefunctions not tapering to 0 and therefore not being normalizable. That only occurs when E is a very specific set of values, namely the roots of our TISE; our next task was to solve for those. To do so, we created a function that returned specifically the last entry in our ψ array, which would be at L, the farthest point we evaluated the function at. In hindsight, this is a somewhat wasteful way of evaluating this, since any L sufficiently outside the potential function would suffice to find whether the value was positive or negative. The reason we cared specifically about a value being positive or negative is because when that value switches from positive to negative or negative to positive, it crosses 0 - which is exactly what we want our wavefunction to trend towards. We can then isolate the E value where it crosses “exactly,” or at least as close as we can feasibly get given the precision of our root finder. The root finder we chose was brentq, due to scipy listing it as “a great general-purpose option.” Once we detected a crossing between two different values of E, we ran our root finder and isolated the place where E crossed 0. These values are the roots of our equation, and what we eventually end up solving for and plotting.
        
However, we ran into another issue, namely one of normalization. We were getting wavefunction solutions, but they all had the exact same amplitude. To fix this, we created a function that would normalize the given wavefunctions by integrating ψ2 wrt x from 0 to E + 3. To be honest, this was a bit of a hack from us since this style of normalization only really works in the triangular potential correctly - but we were struggling with how to set it up otherwise. In this specific case, the bounds of integration are from the left hand side of the well, at 0, to the right hand side, which since we know the rhs is E = x, we can just choose E + 3 to ensure we get an upper bound where the wavefunction is outside the well and has therefore asymptoted towards 0. Once we found the integral of that function, we could divide our ψ values by the square root of that integral to get their normalization constant, finally giving us normalized wavefunctions. Once normalized, our wavefunctions were accurate to what we wanted to obtain initially, and we could get to graphing.
        
We graphed our wavefunctions in two different potentials: the triangular potential, V = x, and the infinite square well, where V = 0 inside a given range L and is infinite outside of it. In the triangular potential, we solved for and graphed the first fifteen E roots, noting that they started out around 1.88 and the distances between them shrunk as the value of E grew. However, in the infinite square well, the distances between roots of E actually increased for higher values of E, likely due to the confined nature of the potential function. 
        
Overall, we found this project to be very enlightening, especially when it came to using RK4 for systems of functions, applying functions at scale, and graphing/ normalizing them in a way that easily communicates our results. We believe we have accomplished what we set out to do for this project in full, and are proud of what we have created.
